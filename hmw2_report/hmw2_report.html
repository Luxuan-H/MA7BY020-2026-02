1 — Introduction

This report applies matrix factorisation methods (PCA, CCA) and clustering algorithms to analyse the voting behaviour of polling stations in Lyon across nine elections held between 2014 and 2022. The goal is to identify the main axes of political variation across Lyon’s 294 polling stations, to measure the stability of voting geography across elections of different types and different years, and to group stations by their political profiles — providing a structured foundation for the regression analysis that predicts the first-round results of the 2024 legislative elections.

The remainder of this section describes the data collection and preparation pipeline. Section 2 presents the analyses in four parts: PCA, CCA, clustering, and regression.

1.2 — Data pipeline

All nine datasets are downloaded as .parquet files from hub.huwise.com and saved locally in data_raw/. Files already present are not re-downloaded.

The elections covered are:

  • European Parliament 2014 — list-based proportional election
  • Presidential 2017 — rounds 1 and 2
  • Legislative 2017 — rounds 1 and 2
  • Municipal 2020 — rounds 1 and 2
  • Presidential 2022 — rounds 1 and 2

These elections were chosen for several reasons. First, the data are freely accessible on the City of Lyon’s open data platform. Second, covering an eight-year span, they encompass all major election types organised in France under universal suffrage (presidential, legislative, municipal, European), which allows for comparisons both within and across election types. Together they yield 294 polling stations and nine observation matrices.

Round 2 elections are excluded from the PCA and subsequent analyses, as second-round ballots typically reduce to two candidates, leaving the five-bloc structure incomplete and making cross-election comparisons unreliable.

Raw candidate names and party codes differ across datasets. All candidates are mapped to one of five unified political blocs, following the French Ministry of the Interior’s political affiliation records at the time of each election:

Bloc Description
extreme_gauche Far left (e.g. Arthaud, Poutou, Roussel)
gauche Left (e.g. Hamon, Mélenchon, Jadot)
centre Centre (e.g. Macron, Lassalle)
droite Right (e.g. Fillon, Pécresse, Dupont-Aignan)
extreme_droite Far right (e.g. Le Pen, Zemmour)

This classification inevitably involves a degree of subjectivity: some candidates are difficult to categorise unambiguously (for example, Dupont-Aignan oscillates between droite and extreme_droite depending on the election cycle), and political affiliations can shift over time. This limitation is acknowledged throughout the analysis, and caution should be exercised when interpreting results that hinge on the assignment of a specific candidate to a given bloc.

For each election, raw vote counts are aggregated by polling station and bloc, then converted to vote shares (proportions summing to 1 per station). This produces one wide table per election, where each row is a polling station and each column is a bloc’s vote share. These share matrices serve as the common input format for all subsequent analyses.

2 — Data Analysis

2.1 — PCA: Principal Component Analysis

PCA was applied to identify the main dimensions of political variation across Lyon’s polling stations. For each election, a matrix of vote shares (stations × blocs) was constructed, centred, and scaled before running PCA via prcomp(). Two visualisations are presented in this section: a scree plot computed on a merged matrix of all five elections, and a stacked bar chart comparing the variance explained by PC1 and PC2 across individual elections.

PC3 explains only 8.1%, and the remaining components each contribute less than 5% (PC4: 4.1%, PC5: 2.9%, PC6: 2.3%).

The cumulative variance curve shows that around five to six components are needed to reach the 80% threshold. This means PC1 and PC2 capture the two dominant axes of political variation, but they do not represent the majority of total variance in the merged dataset.

2.1.2 — PC1+PC2 Variance Explained per Election

When PCA is run separately for each election, the combined variance explained by PC1 and PC2 is higher than in the merged matrix. All five elections exceed the 70% reference line. The values range from 70.9% (Leg 2017 R1) to 82.4% (Pres 2022 R1). This shows that within a single election, political variation across polling stations is more structured than the merged result suggests.

The split between PC1 and PC2 also differs across election types. In presidential elections, PC1 is dominant. Pres 2022 R1 has PC1 at 64% and PC2 at only 18.4%. Pres 2017 R1 shows a similar pattern, with PC1 at 54.1% and PC2 at 23.6%. This suggests that in presidential elections, one axis accounts for most of the variation between polling stations. In legislative and municipal elections, the split is more balanced. Leg 2017 R1 has PC1 at 40.2% and PC2 at 30.7%. Mun 2020 R1 has PC1 at 41.5% and PC2 at 33.7%. This indicates that a second dimension of variation plays a larger role in these election types.

The lower total in the merged scree plot reflects the added complexity of combining elections with different political structures into one matrix. It does not indicate a weakness in using two components for individual elections.

2.1.3 — Correlation Circles

The correlation circles show the loadings of each bloc on PC1 and PC2. Across most elections, gauche and droite point in roughly opposite directions on PC1, consistent with a left–right interpretation of the first axis. Note that PCA loadings are sign-arbitrary, so apparent direction reversals across elections reflect axis flips rather than structural change. A notable pattern appears in Leg 2017 R1 and Mun 2020 R1:

extreme_gauche and extreme_droite point in similar directions on PC2, while mainstream blocs point in the opposite direction. This suggests that in these elections, PC2 captures an extremes-versus-mainstream axis rather than a secondary left–right distinction. This is consistent with the higher PC2 values observed for these two elections in Figure 2. The position of centre varies across elections. In Pres 2022 R1 it is close to droite, but in Pres 2017 R1 it occupies a more independent position. The divers category, present in EU 2014 only, aggregates candidates not assigned to any main bloc and should be treated as a residual category.

2.1.4 — Biplots

In EU 2014 (PC1: 52.4%, PC2: 24.1%), extreme_droite loads strongly on the negative PC2 axis, pointing in a different direction from all other blocs. This pattern does not appear in any other election.

In Pres 2017 R1 (PC1: 54.1%, PC2: 23.6%), centre points toward the upper-left, away from droite. The two blocs occupy clearly separate positions in biplot space.

In Leg 2017 R1 (PC1: 40.2%, PC2: 30.7%), extreme_gauche and extreme_droite both point upward along PC2. gauche and droite are separated along PC1. The station cloud is more spread in both directions than in presidential elections.

Mun 2020 R1 (PC1: 41.5%, PC2: 33.7%) shows a similar pattern to Leg 2017. Both extreme blocs again point upward along PC2, while gauche and droite separate along PC1.

In Pres 2022 R1 (PC1: 64%, PC2: 18.4%), the station cloud is almost entirely spread along the horizontal axis. centre and droite point in nearly the same direction. The left-right structure is the most concentrated of all five elections.

Across all five elections, stations from the 1st arrondissement (01xx) consistently appear on the gauche side, while 6th arrondissement stations (06xx) consistently appear on the droite side. This suggests that the broad political geography of Lyon’s neighbourhoods is stable across election types and years.

2.2 — CCA: Canonical Correlation Analysis

CCA was used to measure the extent to which the voting geographies of two elections are linearly related. Before running CCA, the last column of each share matrix was dropped to remove the linear constraint that vote shares sum to 1. The first canonical correlation r₁ represents the maximum linear association between a weighted combination of blocs in one election and a weighted combination in the other. Values close to 1 suggest strong geographic stability between the two elections. Lower values suggest more substantial restructuring.

Five comparisons were selected:

  1. Municipal 2020 R1 vs. R2 : within-election round consistency. Note that R2 is included here because CCA only requires the reduced bloc matrix, which remains valid with two rounds even when the number of competing lists differs.
  2. Presidential 2017 R1 vs. Presidential 2022 R1 : stability across two presidential elections
  3. Presidential 2017 R1 vs. Legislative 2017 R1 : stability across election types within the same year
  4. European 2014 vs. Presidential 2017 R1 : cross-year, cross-type comparison
  5. European 2014 vs. Municipal 2020 R1 : a longer temporal horizon comparing two list-based elections

The CCA summary table records r₁ and the number of common polling stations for each comparison. The correlation circle plots show, for each election in a pair, how strongly each bloc’s vote share correlates with the first two canonical dimensions. Unlike PCA correlation circles, which reflect the internal structure of a single election matrix, these reflect the shared structure between two matrices.

2.2.1 — Municipal 2020: Round 1 vs. Round 2

The first canonical correlation between Mun 2020 R1 and R2 is r1=0.895, indicating strong geographic stability between the two rounds. The second canonical correlation is much lower (r2=0.495), suggesting that the shared structure between rounds is largely captured by a single dimension. The correlation circles show that in R2, only three blocs are present: gauche, droite, and centre. The extreme_gauche and extreme_droite arrows disappear, consistent with the consolidation of lists between rounds. Despite this reduction, the directions of gauche, droite, and centre remain broadly consistent across the two panels. This confirms that the neighbourhood-level geographic structure of political preferences is preserved between rounds, even as the number of competing lists decreases.

2.2.2 — Presidential 2017 R1 vs. Presidential 2022 R1

The first canonical correlation between Pres 2017 R1 and Pres 2022 R1 is r1=0.929, higher than the within-election baseline of 0.895 from the municipal comparison. The second canonical correlation is r2=0.775, also substantially higher than in the previous comparison. This indicates that the voting geography of presidential elections is highly stable across both dimensions, even over a five-year period. The most visible structural change in the correlation circles is the repositioning of centre. In Pres 2017 R1, centre points toward the upper-left, in a direction clearly separate from droite. In Pres 2022 R1, centre shifts to the upper-right, where it is nearly parallel to droite. This suggests that the geographic correlation between centrist and right-wing vote shares increased between the two elections. By contrast, gauche and extreme_gauche maintain similar directions across the two panels, suggesting that the geographic structure of left-wing voting was more stable over this period.

2.2.3 — Presidential 2017 R1 vs. Legislative 2017 R1

The first canonical correlation between Pres 2017 R1 and Leg 2017 R1 is r1=0.956, with r2=0.933. These are the highest values across all five comparisons in this analysis, higher than both the within-election municipal comparison and the cross-year presidential comparison. This suggests that neighbourhood-level political preferences are strongly preserved across election types, even when the institutional rules differ. The correlation circles show that gauche and droite maintain consistent directions across both panels, indicating that the geographic structure of mainstream left and right voting is stable between the two election types. The position of centre shifts between panels: in Leg 2017 R1 it points toward the upper-right, while in Pres 2017 R1 it shifts toward the lower-right. The largest directional difference is in extreme_droite, which points upper-left in Leg 2017 R1 but more directly upward in Pres 2017 R1. This suggests that the geographic profile of far-right voting was the least stable component across the two election types in 2017.

2.2.4 — European 2014 vs. Presidential 2017 R1

The first canonical correlation between EU 2014 and Pres 2017 R1 is r1=0.967, with r2=0.894. These are the highest values in this analysis, higher than the within-year Pres vs Leg comparison despite a three-year gap and a change in election type. This suggests that the geographic structure of political preferences in Lyon is highly stable across this period. The correlation circles show that droite maintains a consistent direction in both panels, pointing strongly along the negative canonical dim 1 axis. extreme_droite also points in a similar direction in both panels, toward the negative canonical dim 2 axis. These two blocs show the most stable geographic profiles across the two elections. The divers category appears only in the EU 2014 panel. Its arrow is short, suggesting it contributes little to the canonical structure and can be treated as a residual. The most visible change is in centre, which shifts position relative to gauche between the two panels. In EU 2014, centre and gauche point in clearly different directions. In Pres 2017 R1, their relative positions change, consistent with the broader repositioning of the centrist bloc observed in other comparisons.

2.2.5 — European 2014 vs. Municipal 2020 R1

The first canonical correlation between EU 2014 and Mun 2020 R1 is r1=0.937, with r2=0.826. This is the widest temporal gap in the analysis at six years. The r1 value is lower than the EU 2014 vs Pres 2017 R1 comparison (0.967), suggesting a modest reduction in geographic stability over the longer interval. extreme_droite is the most consistent bloc across both panels, pointing strongly upward along canonical dim 2 in both elections. droite and gauche also maintain broadly similar directions across the two panels, indicating that the geographic structure of mainstream left and right voting is preserved over this period. The most visible change is in centre. In EU 2014 it points toward the lower-left, close to droite. In Mun 2020 R1 it shifts upward, moving away from droite and toward a more independent position. extreme_gauche also shifts, pointing more horizontally in EU 2014 and rotating upward in Mun 2020 R1. The divers category appears only in the EU 2014 panel with a short arrow, consistent with its treatment as a residual category throughout this analysis.

2.3 — Clustering

The objective of the clustering analysis is to group Lyon’s polling stations into a small number of politically coherent profiles, to characterise the spatial structure of voting behaviour, and to provide a discrete categorical variable for the regression analysis in Section 2.4. The clustering was performed on a combined feature matrix pooling the vote shares from three elections: Presidential 2017 R1, Municipal 2020 R1, and Presidential 2022 R1. These three elections were selected because they are the most temporally proximate to the 2024 legislative elections used as the regression outcome. The combined matrix describes each polling station in terms of its political profile across three elections spanning roughly five years. Two families of methods were applied: k-means clustering and hierarchical clustering. For k-means, the optimal number of clusters was selected via silhouette analysis. For hierarchical clustering, four combinations of distance metric and linkage method were tested. All solutions were compared using the Adjusted Rand Index (ARI), which measures agreement between two clustering solutions, with values close to 1 indicating strong agreement and values close to 0 indicating agreement no better than chance.

2.3.1 — Silhouette Analysis and Choice of k

Clustering matrix: 293 stations x 15 variables
Optimal k by silhouette: 2

The silhouette plot displays the mean silhouette width for k ranging from 2 to 7. The mean silhouette width measures how much more similar each station is to its own cluster than to the nearest alternative cluster. Higher values indicate more compact and well-separated clusters.

The highest value is observed at k=2 (mean silhouette width ≈ 0.42), which is selected as the reference solution. The score drops to 0.334 at k=3 and continues to decrease for larger values of k , with a small local increase at k=5 (0.292) before declining again. The clear peak at k=2 suggests that a two-cluster partition best separates Lyon’s polling stations under this criterion.

It should be noted that silhouette analysis identifies the most internally coherent partition given the data and the Euclidean distance metric. The selected k is therefore used as a working hypothesis rather than a definitive answer.

2.4 — Regression: Predicting the 2024 Legislative Elections

The regression analysis uses the political geography characterised in the preceding sections to predict the first-round results of the 2024 legislative elections in Lyon’s polling stations. This constitutes the main predictive objective of the project.

2.4.1 — Data preparation

Two additional datasets were acquired for this section: the European 2024 results and the Legislative 2024 R1 results, both filtered to Lyon (commune code 69123) from CSV files published by the French Ministry of the Interior. The nuance codes follow the official classification system used in the earlier elections and were mapped to the same five-bloc scheme. The European 2024 results serve as an additional explanatory variable. The Legislative 2024 R1 results are the response variable.

The regression dataset was constructed by joining all wide share tables on polling station identifier (bureau). After joining, the dataset contains 283 polling stations with complete observations across all elections. The cluster label from the k=2 k-means solution (Section 2.3) was joined as a two-level categorical variable (C1 and C2), with C1 serving as the reference category. This variable provides a discrete summary of each station’s historical political profile: centre-right leaning (C1) versus left-leaning (C2).

2.4.2 — Model specification

One linear regression model was estimated per political bloc:

\[\hat{y}_{\text{leg2024},b} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{\text{eu14},b} + \beta_2 x_{\text{p17r1},b} + \beta_3 x_{\text{leg17r1},b} + \beta_4 x_{\text{mun20r1},b} + \beta_5 x_{\text{p22r1},b} + \beta_6 x_{\text{euro2024},b} + \gamma_k + \varepsilon\]

where \(b\) denotes the political bloc, \(x_{e,b}\) is the vote share for bloc \(b\) in election \(e\), and \(\gamma_k\) is a fixed effect for the cluster \(k\) to which the station belongs. The cluster fixed effect captures systematic differences in baseline vote levels across neighbourhood types that are not explained by the bloc-specific temporal predictors alone.

It should be noted that this specification treats each bloc independently: vote shares across blocs within a station are not constrained to sum to 1 in the regression. This simplification allows each model to be estimated and interpreted separately, but may introduce some inconsistency if predictions are aggregated.

2.4.3 — R² per bloc

The bar chart shows the R² for each of the five bloc-specific models. Gauche achieves the best fit (R²=0.966), followed by extreme_droite (0.935), centre (0.874), and droite (0.829). These four blocs are classified as excellent fit. Extreme_gauche is a clear outlier at R²=0.437, the only bloc classified as moderate, indicating that far-left vote shares in 2024 are less well predicted by the historical pattern of explanatory variables.

These figures should be interpreted cautiously. A high R² indicates good in-sample fit but does not guarantee out-of-sample accuracy. With seven predictors and 283 observations, some degree of overfitting is plausible.

2.4.4 — Coefficient plots

For each bloc, the coefficient plot shows the estimated regression coefficients and their 95% confidence intervals. Coefficients statistically significant at the 5% level are shown in blue; non-significant coefficients are shown in grey. A coefficient to the right of zero indicates a positive association between that election’s bloc share and the 2024 legislative outcome. The most recent elections tend to carry the largest predictive weight, but the pattern varies across blocs, as discussed below.

For gauche, centre, and extreme_droite, Pres 2022 R1 is the dominant predictor with a large positive significant coefficient. Euro 2024 adds a smaller but significant positive contribution in all three models.

For gauche and centre, Mun 2020 R1 is also positive and significant.

However, earlier elections show negative significant coefficients in several models: eu14 is negative and significant for gauche and droite, and leg17r1 is negative and significant for centre. This suggests that in the presence of more recent predictors, earlier elections contribute negatively to the fit.

For droite, Euro 2024 is by far the dominant predictor. Pres 2022 R1 is not statistically significant, which distinguishes this bloc from the others and suggests that the geographic structure of right-wing voting shifted more substantially between 2022 and 2024.

For extreme_gauche, the strongest predictor is Pres 2017 R1. Leg 2017 R1 is negative and significant. Several other predictors are also significant, but in different directions, which is consistent with the low R²=0.437 for this bloc. The cluster fixed effect (C2) is significant in two models only: positive for gauche and negative for extreme_droite. For the remaining three blocs it is not statistically significant, suggesting that the cluster label adds little predictive information beyond the bloc-specific vote share history.

The regression results confirm that Lyon’s political geography, as characterised in the preceding sections, provides strong predictive power for the 2024 legislative first round. Four of the five bloc-specific models achieve R² above 0.82, with gauche reaching R²=0.966. This is consistent with the high canonical correlations observed in the CCA analysis and the stable cluster structure identified in Section 2.3: neighbourhoods that leaned left or centre-right in earlier elections continued to do so in 2024. The most recent elections, particularly Pres 2022 R1 and Euro 2024, carry the largest predictive weight in most models, suggesting that geographic preferences are both stable over time and sensitive to the most recent political context.

Extreme_gauche is the clear exception, with R²=0.437. This is consistent with the lower geographic stability of far-left voting observed across the biplot and CCA analyses.

The model does not account for changes in which candidates stood in each polling station’s constituency between elections. Only electoral data are used; sociodemographic variables would likely enrich the model.

make_wide <- function(df, label) {
  df |>
    dplyr::group_by(bureau, nuance) |>
    dplyr::summarise(votes = sum(votes, na.rm = TRUE), .groups = "drop") |>
    dplyr::group_by(bureau) |>
    dplyr::mutate(pct = votes / sum(votes)) |>
    dplyr::ungroup() |>
    dplyr::select(bureau, nuance, pct) |>
    tidyr::pivot_wider(names_from = nuance, values_from = pct, values_fill = 0) |>
    dplyr::rename_with(~ paste0(label, "_", .), -bureau) |>
    dplyr::select(where(~ !all(. == 0)))
}

#  II. CCA — CANONICAL CORRELATION ANALYSIS
# ============================================================

run_cca <- function(m1, m2, tag1, tag2) {
  
  tag <- paste0(tag1, "_vs_", tag2)
  
  # Step 1: find common stations and align row order
  common_ids <- intersect(rownames(m1), rownames(m2))
  m1 <- m1[match(common_ids, rownames(m1)), , drop = FALSE]
  m2 <- m2[match(common_ids, rownames(m2)), , drop = FALSE]
  rownames(m1) <- common_ids
  rownames(m2) <- common_ids
  message("CCA ", tag, ": ", length(common_ids), " common polling stations")

  # Step 2: drop last column to remove linear dependency (shares sum to 1)
  m1_cca <- m1[, -ncol(m1), drop = FALSE]
  m2_cca <- m2[, -ncol(m2), drop = FALSE]

  # Step 3: filter zero-variance and NA/Inf columns for cancor() only
  keep1 <- apply(m1_cca, 2, function(x)
    !any(is.na(x) | is.nan(x) | is.infinite(x)) && var(x) > 0)
  keep2 <- apply(m2_cca, 2, function(x)
    !any(is.na(x) | is.nan(x) | is.infinite(x)) && var(x) > 0)
  m1_cca <- m1_cca[, keep1, drop = FALSE]
  m2_cca <- m2_cca[, keep2, drop = FALSE]

  message("  m1 valid columns (", ncol(m1_cca), "): ",
          paste(colnames(m1_cca), collapse = ", "))
  message("  m2 valid columns (", ncol(m2_cca), "): ",
          paste(colnames(m2_cca), collapse = ", "))

  # Step 4: skip if either matrix has 0 columns
  if (ncol(m1_cca) == 0 || ncol(m2_cca) == 0) {
    warning("CCA ", tag, " skipped: no valid columns after filtering.")
    return(invisible(NULL))
  }

  # Step 5: run CCA
  cca <- stats::cancor(m1_cca, m2_cca)

  # Align coefficient directions for interpretability
  for (d in seq_along(cca$cor)) {
    f <- sign(sum(cca$xcoef[, d] * cca$ycoef[, d]))
    cca$xcoef[, d] <- cca$xcoef[, d] * f
    cca$ycoef[, d] <- cca$ycoef[, d] * f
  }

  # Step 6: compute canonical scores using filtered matrices
  n_can <- min(2, ncol(cca$xcoef), ncol(cca$ycoef))
  U <- m1_cca %*% cca$xcoef[, seq_len(n_can), drop = FALSE]
  V <- m2_cca %*% cca$ycoef[, seq_len(n_can), drop = FALSE]

  # Step 7: correlate full matrices with canonical scores (all blocs shown in plot)
  cor_mat1 <- cor(m1, U)
  colnames(cor_mat1) <- paste0("dim", seq_len(n_can))
  cor_mat2 <- cor(m2, V)
  colnames(cor_mat2) <- paste0("dim", seq_len(n_can))

  # If only one canonical dimension, add dim2 = 0 so ggplot can render
  if (!"dim2" %in% colnames(cor_mat1)) {
    cor_mat1 <- cbind(cor_mat1, dim2 = 0)
    cor_mat2 <- cbind(cor_mat2, dim2 = 0)
  }

  df_plot <- dplyr::bind_rows(
    as.data.frame(cor_mat1) |>
      tibble::rownames_to_column("variable") |>
      dplyr::mutate(election = tag1),
    as.data.frame(cor_mat2) |>
      tibble::rownames_to_column("variable") |>
      dplyr::mutate(election = tag2)
  ) |>
  dplyr::mutate(
    nuance_raw = stringr::str_remove(variable, "^[^_]+_"),
    nuance = dplyr::case_when(
      stringr::str_detect(nuance_raw, "extreme_gauche")                      ~ "Ext.gauche",
      stringr::str_detect(nuance_raw, "gauche") &
        !stringr::str_detect(nuance_raw, "extreme")                          ~ "Gauche",
      stringr::str_detect(nuance_raw, "centre")                              ~ "Centre",
      stringr::str_detect(nuance_raw, "droite") &
        !stringr::str_detect(nuance_raw, "extreme")                          ~ "Droite",
      stringr::str_detect(nuance_raw, "extreme_droite")                      ~ "Ext.droite",
      TRUE                                                                   ~ "Divers"
    )
  )

  circle <- tibble::tibble(
    x = cos(seq(0, 2 * pi, length.out = 300)),
    y = sin(seq(0, 2 * pi, length.out = 300))
  )

  color_palette <- c(
    "Ext.gauche" = "#B22222", "Gauche" = "#E15759",
    "Centre"     = "#F28E2B", "Droite" = "#4E79A7",
    "Ext.droite" = "#1a1a6e", "Divers" = "#76B7B2"
  )

#Plot 9/10/11/12/13: CCA correlation circle plot  
  p <- ggplot2::ggplot(
    df_plot, ggplot2::aes(x = dim1, y = dim2, colour = nuance)
  ) +
    ggplot2::geom_path(
      data = circle,
      ggplot2::aes(x = x, y = y),
      colour = "grey70", inherit.aes = FALSE
    ) +
    ggplot2::geom_hline(yintercept = 0, linetype = "dashed", colour = "grey60") +
    ggplot2::geom_vline(xintercept = 0, linetype = "dashed", colour = "grey60") +
    ggplot2::geom_segment(
      ggplot2::aes(x = 0, y = 0, xend = dim1, yend = dim2),
      arrow = ggplot2::arrow(length = ggplot2::unit(0.15, "cm")),
      linewidth = 0.8
    ) +
    ggrepel::geom_text_repel(
      ggplot2::aes(label = variable),
      size = 3.5, fontface = "bold",
      max.overlaps = 20, show.legend = FALSE
    ) +
    ggplot2::facet_wrap(~ election) +
    ggplot2::scale_color_manual(values = color_palette, name = "Nuance") +
    ggplot2::coord_fixed(xlim = c(-1.05, 1.05), ylim = c(-1.05, 1.05)) +
    ggplot2::labs(
      title    = paste0("CCA — ", tag),
      subtitle = paste0(
        "r1 = ", round(cca$cor[1], 3),
        " | r2 = ", round(cca$cor[2], 3)
      ),
      x = paste0("Canonical dim 1  (r = ", round(cca$cor[1], 3), ")"),
      y = paste0("Canonical dim 2  (r = ", round(cca$cor[2], 3), ")")
    ) +
    ggplot2::theme_minimal(base_size = 12) +
    ggplot2::theme(legend.position = "bottom")

ggsave(paste0("figures/cca_", tag, ".png"), plot = p, width = 10, height = 6, dpi = 150)
  #print(p)

  message("CCA done: ", tag,
          " | r1 = ", round(cca$cor[1], 3),
          " | r2 = ", round(cca$cor[2], 3))

  invisible(list(cca = cca, plot = p, n_stations = length(common_ids)))
}

run_hclust <- function(dist_method, link_method, k, tag) {
  d  <- dist(X_cluster, method = dist_method)
  hc <- hclust(d, method = link_method)
  clusters <- cutree(hc, k = k)
  # Plot18/19/20/21 dendrogram
  png(paste0("figures/dendrogram_", tag, ".png"), width = 1000, height = 600)
  plot(hc, labels = FALSE,
       main = paste0("Dendrogram — dist=", dist_method,
                     " | link=", link_method, " | k=", k),
       xlab = "", sub = "")
  rect.hclust(hc, k = k, border = "red")
  dev.off()   
  message("HClust done: dist=", dist_method,
          " | link=", link_method, " | k=", k,
          " | tag=", tag)
  invisible(clusters)
}

run_bloc_regression <- function(bloc, data) {

  y_col <- paste0("leg2024_", bloc)

  # Explanatory variables: same bloc from each past election + cluster
  x_cols <- c(
    paste0("eu14_",     bloc),
    paste0("p17r1_",    bloc),
    paste0("leg17r1_",  bloc),
    paste0("mun20r1_",  bloc),
    paste0("p22r1_",    bloc),
    paste0("euro2024_", bloc),
    "cluster"
  )

  # Keep only columns that exist in the data
  x_cols <- x_cols[x_cols %in% colnames(data)]

  formula <- as.formula(paste(y_col, "~", paste(x_cols, collapse = " + ")))
  model   <- lm(formula, data = data)

  list(bloc = bloc, model = model, summary = summary(model))
}

plot_coefficients <- function(reg_model_item) {
  coef_df <- broom::tidy(reg_model_item$model, conf.int = TRUE) |>
    dplyr::filter(term != "(Intercept)") |>
    dplyr::mutate(
      significant = p.value < 0.05,
      term        = stringr::str_remove(term, "cluster")
    )
  p <- ggplot2::ggplot(coef_df,
                  ggplot2::aes(x = reorder(term, estimate),
                               y = estimate,
                               colour = significant)) +
    ggplot2::geom_hline(yintercept = 0, linetype = "dashed", colour = "grey60") +
    ggplot2::geom_pointrange(
      ggplot2::aes(ymin = conf.low, ymax = conf.high),
      linewidth = 0.8, size = 0.6
    ) +
    ggplot2::scale_colour_manual(
      values = c("TRUE" = "#4E79A7", "FALSE" = "grey70"),
      labels = c("TRUE" = "p < 0.05", "FALSE" = "p ≥ 0.05"),
      name   = "Significance"
    ) +
    ggplot2::coord_flip() +
    ggplot2::labs(
      title    = paste0("Coefficients — ", reg_model_item$bloc),
      subtitle = paste0("R² = ", round(summary(reg_model_item$model)$r.squared, 3)),
      x        = "",
      y        = "Coefficient (95% CI)"
    ) +
    ggplot2::theme_minimal(base_size = 12) +
    ggplot2::theme(plot.title       = ggplot2::element_text(face = "bold"),
                   plot.subtitle    = ggplot2::element_text(colour = "grey40"),
                   legend.position  = "right",
                   panel.grid.minor = ggplot2::element_blank())
  ggsave(paste0("figures/coef_", reg_model_item$bloc, ".png"),
         plot = p, width = 9, height = 6, dpi = 150)
  return(p)
}
